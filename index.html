Economic growth and human well-being

Richer countries usually live better, but not always by as much as their income would suggest. We measure, for 148 countries from 2000 to 2022, how far well-being sits above or below what countries of similar income achieve, and ask what explains the difference.

Luyu Su and Yifan Zuo, STATS 401 final project, interim check-in. See the original proposal and the repository.

0Changes since the proposal

While building the dataset we found that two proposed views (the quadrant scatterplot and the clustered bubble map) answered the same question with the same axes, and that the radar chart with adjustable weights had no clear task behind it. We reorganised the project around one derived measure, the well-being gap: a country's well-being score minus the score expected for its income in that year. Every view now answers a different question about that gap.

Processing completed

  1. Harmonise and integrate. All sources were joined on ISO-3 codes into one country-year panel. We kept the years every source shares (2000–2022) and sovereign countries with at least 1 million people.
  2. Fill short gaps and flag them. Interior gaps were linearly interpolated within each country. At the edges, slow-moving coverage indicators (water, sanitation, electricity, schooling) were carried forward up to six years and all others up to three; this mainly extends PM2.5 from 2020 to 2022. 734 cells were filled, and each row carries an n_imputed count. Countries without GDP data or missing more than one of ten indicators in any year were dropped (Cuba, Djibouti, Eritrea, Hong Kong, Nicaragua, North Korea, Somalia, South Sudan, Venezuela, Yemen).
  3. Rescale with fixed goalposts. Each indicator was mapped to 0–100 using the same bounds in every year, so scores are comparable over time. We used the UNDP HDI goalposts where they exist, a log scale for child mortality and PM2.5, the WHO guideline (5 µg/m³) as the best air-quality value, and reversed the scale for indicators where lower is better.
  4. Aggregate into five dimensions. Health (life expectancy, under-5 mortality), education (mean and expected schooling), living conditions (water, sanitation, electricity), work (unemployment, vulnerable employment) and clean air (PM2.5). The well-being score is the mean of the five. CO₂ per capita is kept separately as an environmental cost rather than a component.
  5. Derive the well-being gap. For each year we fit a LOWESS curve of well-being on log GDP per capita across all countries. The gap is the vertical distance from that curve; we repeat this for each dimension.
  6. Test weight sensitivity. We recomputed every 2022 gap under 2,000 random dimension weightings (Dirichlet, α = 2) and stored the 5th–95th percentile of each country's gap and rank.
  7. Cluster development profiles. K-means (k = 5) on log GDP plus the five dimensions, fitted jointly on 2000, 2008, 2015 and 2022 so each cluster means the same thing in every year.

A first sanity check: across 2,053 country-years the well-being score correlates with WHR life satisfaction at r = 0.78, and, more tellingly, the income-adjusted gap correlates with income-adjusted life satisfaction at r = 0.28. Countries that beat their income on our measure also tend to report higher life satisfaction than their income predicts.

Known limitations. PM2.5 exposure includes natural desert dust, which penalises Gulf and Sahel countries heavily (visible in Figure 4). A collapse in GDP, as in Syria, can produce a positive gap because services built earlier outlast income. Education data for 2022 are partly UNDP projections.

3Interaction and animation plan

The final site will be a set of coordinated views around one shared state: the selected year, the selected countries, and the current dimension weights. Selecting a country or a flow in any view highlights it in all others, so a user can go from "Qatar is last" (Figure 3) to "because of air and education" (Figure 4) to "its development profile never changed" (Figure 5) without losing context.

4Evaluation plan

What we will evaluate

Three things, following the levels of Munzner's nested model: whether the derived measures are valid (data abstraction), whether users can complete the intended tasks correctly and quickly (task and idiom), and whether the coordinated views are usable (interaction).

How we will evaluate it

Analytical checks, partly done. Correlation of the score and the gap with WHR life satisfaction (done: r = 0.78 and 0.28). Weight robustness of rankings (done, shown in Figure 3). Still to do: re-run the gap with a different LOWESS bandwidth (0.4 and 0.8) and with a linear fit, and report how many countries change sign; drop the clean-air dimension to measure how much desert dust drives the Gulf result.

Task-based user study. Eight to ten participants (classmates and friends outside statistics) complete about eight tasks mapped to the research questions, for example "Which of these two countries is further above its income expectation?", "Name one dimension that explains Nigeria's gap", "Did more countries move up or down between 2008 and 2015?". Each participant works with the interactive site and thinks aloud; half do the first four tasks on static versions of the figures first, so we can compare static against interactive.

Expert feedback. A short walkthrough with the instructor or TA and one person with a development-economics background, focused on whether the gap measure and profile labels are fair and not misleading.

Data and feedback we will collect

Task accuracy and completion time; a 1–5 confidence rating per task; the System Usability Scale after the session; notes of where participants hesitated or misread a chart; and a simple interaction log (which views and controls were used, in what order). We will use the results to fix the two or three largest problems before the final presentation.
