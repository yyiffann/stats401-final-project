Economic growth and human well-being

Richer countries usually live better, but not always by as much as their income would suggest. We measure, for 148 countries from 2000 to 2022, how far well-being sits above or below what countries of similar income achieve, and ask what explains the difference.

0Why this matters, and what we ask

Why

GDP per person is the default yardstick of national progress, yet income says little about whether people are healthy, schooled, safely supplied with water and power, in work and breathing clean air. Where well-being follows income, growth is a fair proxy for progress. Where it does not, we want to know who is ahead, who is behind and why.

What

A country-year panel of 148 countries, 2000–2022: GDP per capita and ten well-being indicators from the World Bank and UNDP, combined into five dimensions and one score. The key derived measure is the well-being gap: a country's score minus the score expected for its income that year.

How

Five coordinated views move from where, to how, to who, to why, to who moved. One shared state (year, selected countries, dimension weights) links them, so a question raised in one view can be followed up in the next.

Research questions

  1. How does economic prosperity vary across countries and over time? Figure 1
  2. How have countries followed different paths of economic and human development? Figure 2
  3. To what extent is higher GDP associated with higher well-being, and who departs from that pattern? Figures 2 and 3
  4. Which dimensions contribute to the differences between countries? Figure 4
  5. Which countries moved to a higher stage of development, and how many people does that involve? Figure 5

How to read this page. The bar at the top of the page is shared by all views: move the year, add or remove countries. Hovering a country in any view lights it up in all the others; clicking selects it (orange outline). Each figure has its own controls just above it.

→What we learn

  1. Income is a strong but imperfect guide. At the same income, countries differ by dozens of points of well-being: in 2022 the gap runs from +16 (Kyrgyz Republic) to −21 (Qatar).
  2. The same income buys more well-being than it used to. The expected curve rose at every income level between 2000 and 2022, so a country can improve without outgrowing its peers.
  3. Falling short has different causes. Gulf states lose on clean air and education; Sub-Saharan resource exporters lose on health, living conditions and work. A single “resource curse” label hides this.
  4. Overperformance is mostly about basic services and schooling, and the top of the ranking is robust to how well-being is weighted, while some middle positions are not.
  5. Development stages are moving, unevenly. Most “catching up” countries have reached the upper-middle profile since 2000, while fewer than half of the lowest-profile countries have moved up, and counting people instead of countries shows how much China and India shape the global picture.

Processing completed

  1. Harmonise and integrate. All sources were joined on ISO-3 codes into one country-year panel. We kept the years every source shares (2000–2022) and sovereign countries with at least 1 million people.
  2. Fill short gaps and flag them. Interior gaps were linearly interpolated within each country. At the edges, slow-moving coverage indicators (water, sanitation, electricity, schooling) were carried forward up to six years and all others up to three; this mainly extends PM2.5 from 2020 to 2022. 734 cells were filled, and each row carries an n_imputed count. Countries without GDP data or missing more than one of ten indicators in any year were dropped (Cuba, Djibouti, Eritrea, Hong Kong, Nicaragua, North Korea, Somalia, South Sudan, Venezuela, Yemen).
  3. Rescale with fixed goalposts. Each indicator was mapped to 0–100 using the same bounds in every year, so scores are comparable over time. We used the UNDP HDI goalposts where they exist, a log scale for child mortality and PM2.5, the WHO guideline (5 µg/m³) as the best air-quality value, and reversed the scale for indicators where lower is better.
  4. Aggregate into five dimensions. Health (life expectancy, under-5 mortality), education (mean and expected schooling), living conditions (water, sanitation, electricity), work (unemployment, vulnerable employment) and clean air (PM2.5). The well-being score is the mean of the five. CO₂ per capita is kept separately as an environmental cost rather than a component.
  5. Derive the well-being gap. For each year we fit a LOWESS curve of well-being on log GDP per capita across all countries. The gap is the vertical distance from that curve; we repeat this for each dimension.
  6. Test weight sensitivity. We recomputed every 2022 gap under 2,000 random dimension weightings (Dirichlet, α = 2) and stored the 5th–95th percentile of each country's gap and rank.
  7. Cluster development profiles. K-means (k = 5) on log GDP plus the five dimensions, fitted jointly on 2000, 2008, 2015 and 2022 so each cluster means the same thing in every year.

Implementation

The site is plain HTML, CSS and JavaScript with D3.js v7 and topojson-client (both vendored in lib/), so it runs on GitHub Pages with no build step. All five figures read one shared state object (js/common.js): the selected year, the selected countries, the dimension weights and each view's display options. Changing the state in any view redraws only the views that depend on it. Figure 3 recomputes the expected-well-being curve in the browser with a JavaScript port of the same LOWESS fit used in Python (js/lowess.js); for equal weights it reproduces the Python values to within 0.01 points.

Validation: across 2,053 country-years the well-being score correlates with WHR life satisfaction at r = 0.78, and, more tellingly, the income-adjusted gap correlates with income-adjusted life satisfaction at r = 0.28. Countries that beat their income on our measure also tend to report higher life satisfaction than their income predicts.

Known limitations. PM2.5 exposure includes natural desert dust, which penalises Gulf and Sahel countries heavily (visible in Figure 4). A collapse in GDP, as in Syria, can produce a positive gap because services built earlier outlast income. Education data for 2022 are partly UNDP projections.
